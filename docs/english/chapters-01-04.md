# Chapter 1: Introduction: An Invitation to Transformation

## The Fertile Ground of Childhood

Childhood is the most crucial period of human life. It is during childhood that the psyche and the mental apparatus, still in formation, begin to be shaped by the child's experiences. The *ego*, the psychic agency that mediates between our inner impulses and the demands of reality, is still immature and developing. For this reason, interactions and stimuli from the environment become decisive in the construction of identity and personality, and they can influence the individual both positively and negatively. During the first years of life, when everything around us profoundly affects our emotions, fertile ground is formed for the creation of psychological patterns that will affect the entire course of our existence.

Children's emotional vulnerability is a central aspect of this stage. They do not yet possess the symbolic resources needed to process what they experience. When a child faces intense stimuli, such as abandonment, rejection, violence, or neglect, the child's brain does not have the maturity needed to process and understand what is happening. Whatever cannot be understood or assimilated is registered in the unconscious and in the body as an excess, becoming *repressed* (that is, pushed out of conscious awareness) and giving rise to trauma. In this context, trauma is related not only to the event itself, but also to the helplessness experienced, the feeling that the person who should provide protection has become a threat, leaving the child's inner world unstable and unpredictable.

The influences of childhood, including parents, school, friends, and cultural contexts, play a fundamental role in the formation of identity. The bonds formed during this period determine how children perceive themselves and how they will learn to relate to the world. In a harmonious family environment, a child tends to develop a positive view of themselves. Exposure to conflict, neglect, or rejection, however, can result in an emotionally maladjusted adult. The way a child is treated, loved, or rejected directly affects their self-esteem and sense of belonging.

Psychologically speaking, childhood is the period of life most vulnerable to traumatization. A child is not fully able to distinguish "what belongs to someone else" from "what belongs to me." Painful experiences such as rejection or abandonment are often internalized as feelings of guilt: *"If I was rejected, it is because I am not good enough"*; *"If I was abandoned, it is because I do not deserve love."* When left untreated, these feelings become beliefs that structure identity and shape how individuals perceive themselves throughout life. Over time, this internalization contributes to the development of dysfunctional patterns, including anxiety, compulsions, difficulty forming bonds, and even identity disorders in adulthood.

A child's emotional system is still in a raw state, without mature filters. The areas of the brain responsible for emotional regulation and rational thought are still developing, while the *limbic system*, the region of the brain that controls emotional reactions, responds intensely to threatening stimuli. Adverse experiences are therefore felt more intensely and stored more deeply and lastingly in emotional memory. These memories are not simply rational records. They are emotionally charged experiences that shape the child's perception and, later, the adult's.

When trauma occurs, the child resorts to primitive defense mechanisms, such as dissociation, repression, or denial, in an attempt to survive the pain. Although effective during childhood, these mechanisms become dysfunctional in adulthood, manifesting as behavioral patterns that limit development and emotional well-being. Difficulty establishing healthy bonds, constant hypervigilance, and a chronic feeling of emptiness are some of the effects of unresolved trauma. Childhood trauma does not disappear. It reorganizes itself and affects how the individual deals with the surrounding world.

When primary caregiving bonds are disrupted and emotional needs are not met, the child develops mechanisms of self-sufficiency to cope with the absence. A lack of security leads the child to act independently, take on responsibilities too early, and build an identity around the need to be seen and recognized. This process produces an adult who often acts as though they cannot rely on anyone, carrying an unconscious fear of being rejected or neglected. The identity formed in childhood, marked by these emotional barriers, is directly reflected in how the person behaves and relates to others throughout life.

Yet our story does not have to be defined by pain. As the Bible states:

> *"Do not conform to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may discern what is the good, pleasing, and perfect will of God."* (Romans 12:2)

Here the apostle Paul presents not merely an exhortation, but a profound spiritual and psychological calling, a great principle for the transformation of lives. This passage leads us to reflect not only on changing the mind, but also on changing the inner belief system and the way we interpret life and its events.

Mental transformation is not a magical or instantaneous process, but a reinterpretation of past experiences in light of a new outlook on life. Giving new meaning to one's personal history is therefore a path toward healing, allowing a person to break free from the bonds of the past and rebuild their identity in a healthy way.

This message calls for an action that presents a great personal challenge, because change is extremely difficult to achieve. *"Our brain prefers familiar hells to unfamiliar paradises."*

Changes in behavior, beliefs, and mindset are processes in which we often encounter internal resistance. This happens because our minds and bodies are deeply connected to patterns established over time. Imagine that your mind works like a well-defined path through a forest: the more often you travel that path, the clearer and easier it becomes to follow. These neural pathways are formed by our experiences and by the beliefs we acquire throughout life.

When we decide to change, we are essentially trying to create a new path through the forest. The new path must be traveled repeatedly until it becomes as clear as the old one. During the process, we may feel discomfort or frustration because the mind's natural tendency is to return to familiar paths, which require less effort.

Our mind also has a *comfort zone*, a space where it feels safe and familiar. When we try to change something, we challenge that zone, which can generate fear or anxiety. This reaction is a natural defense against the unknown.

The difficulty we encounter when trying to change is not a sign of weakness or inability, but a normal response of the body and mind to something new. The good news is that, like the path through the forest, the new behavior becomes easier as we practice, repeat, and persist. It is like a muscle that must be trained: it takes discipline and good habits to produce hypertrophy. The key is to take small, consistent steps, allowing the mind to adapt gradually.

> *"The place where God is taking you now requires new habits, a new mindset, and constant prayer."* (Billy Graham)

This statement invites us to reflect on the process of inner transformation needed to reach a new stage of spiritual and personal growth. From a Christian perspective, God often guides us to a place of greater maturity, where we are challenged to abandon old patterns and draw closer to Him. This "place" can be understood as a new stage of life, whether a new purpose, a new spiritual understanding, or a new mission.

It is an invitation to dismantle what life built upon dubious and improvised foundations. It is an invitation to those who are truly willing to undertake a difficult but rewarding journey. Change demands conscious effort and perseverance because the brain tends to cling to the comfort of already established routines. The theory of self-control and positive reinforcement is central to this process: the brain must learn that the new behavior also brings a reward, even when that reward is not immediately obvious. Motivation and consistent practice are essential because repetition strengthens new neural connections.

> *"WHEN THE PAIN OF NOT LIVING BECOMES GREATER THAN THE FEAR OF CHANGE, A PERSON CHANGES."* (Sigmund Freud)

New habits are necessary because, to reach a new stage in our journey, we must change the way we behave in daily life. Habits are one of the foundations of our lives, and the Bible teaches us that our actions should reflect our relationship with God, since relationship is one of the divine attributes. When Paul speaks of the importance of "renewing the mind" (Romans 12:2), he indicates that external changes begin with internal transformations. From a psychological perspective, we know that the brain tends to cling to old habits because they are automatic and more energy-efficient. Creating new habits, however, especially when they are aligned with a greater purpose, requires effort, repetition, and consistency.

## The Purpose of This Book

Healing from childhood trauma is possible, but it requires a conscious effort to rewrite one's personal story. The human psyche is resilient and has the capacity to heal, provided that it is given room to do so. By becoming aware of the wounds of the past, individuals can begin to create new memories and experiences that replace the old ones, allowing the pain of trauma to become a source of learning.

It is essential to reflect on how childhood affects the formation of identity and how childhood trauma manifests in adult life. Understanding the origins of dysfunctional behavior and of the emotional patterns formed throughout life is a crucial step toward healing. The process of giving new meaning to one's experiences and renewing the mind is essential if we are to live the abundant life for which we were created. Because Christ offers us a full life, transforming the mind is the key to breaking free from patterns of pain and attaining true emotional freedom.

This book seeks to give readers a deeper understanding of the formation of childhood trauma, its effects in adult life, and the paths toward transformation. By reflecting on these dynamics, we hope readers will be able to identify the patterns that limit their development and, in doing so, open themselves to new possibilities for healing and growth.

*If external changes begin with internal transformations, we must return to the beginning, to that place where everything first began to be written within us. That place is childhood. In the next chapter, we will delve into the concept of the inner child and understand how the earliest wounds take root in the human soul, long before we have words with which to name them.*

# Chapter 2: The Inner Child and the First Wounds

## Back to the Beginning

In the previous chapter, we saw that childhood is the fertile ground where the psychological patterns that accompany us throughout life are formed, and that the renewal of the mind proposed in Romans 12:2 is the path toward transformation. But in order to transform, we must first understand what needs to be transformed. This leads us to a fundamental question: how are our oldest wounds? Before moving forward into the concepts of trauma and the mechanisms of healing, we need to look within, at the part of us that continues to react like the child we once were.

## The Concept of the Inner Child

When we discuss childhood, we are referring not only to children as people outside ourselves, but also to what psychology calls the *inner child*. This concept, found in approaches such as Humanistic Psychology, particularly in the works of Carl Rogers and John Bradshaw, refers to the set of primary emotional experiences, fundamental emotional needs, and formative memories that we carry from our earliest years. Thus, when we ask, "How is our inner child?" we are really asking about the state of our primary emotional wounds and how they influence our adult lives.

Many adults continue to be led by this wounded inner child, marked by emotional neglect and by the absence of attention, protection, affection, boundaries, or validation. Developmental psychology, represented by authors such as Donald Winnicott, John Bowlby, and Mary Ainsworth, demonstrates that a lack of *secure attachment*, meaning a stable and dependable bond with caregivers, creates significant difficulties in adulthood: fear of abandonment, a compulsive search for acceptance, patterns of self-sabotage, relationship difficulties, and rigid defense mechanisms.

These wounds are not merely unresolved emotions. They often become internal structures of functioning that shape how we interpret the world. As a result, we learn to repress legitimate emotions in order to ensure social survival and preserve a supposed "harmony" in the environment in which we were raised. We develop compensatory behaviors, such as perfectionism, excessive docility, aggression, emotional avoidance, or hyper-independence. These served as defense strategies in childhood but become emotional prisons in adult life.

## Early Experiences and the Architecture of the Soul

Child development is the ground on which the architecture of the psyche is built. A baby is not born with a consolidated identity; the baby forms it in the mirror of the gaze that welcomes them. Internal security develops through consistent care, constant presence, and emotional validation.

When this foundation fails, whether through physical absence, emotional unavailability, or the emotional instability of caregivers, a primary experience of helplessness takes root. The child, still unable to process cognitively what is happening, feels before understanding. What the child feels is engraved in the body and the unconscious.

If love is inconsistent, the child learns that they must adapt to survive. If care is cold, the child learns that their emotions are excessive. If the environment is chaotic, the child develops hypervigilance. If expectations are excessive, the child becomes a perfectionist. If there is neglect, the child may become invisible or hyper-independent.

These mechanisms are not moral failings. They are strategies the brain developed for survival. Yet what served as a defense in childhood can become a prison in adult life. The child who had to remain silent to preserve the bond becomes an adult who cannot express their needs. The child who assumed adult responsibilities too early becomes incapable of resting. The child who experienced rejection becomes dependent on approval.

The soul learns to protect itself before it learns to trust.

## Trauma: When Experience Exceeds the Capacity to Process It

Trauma is not only the painful event. It is also the inability to integrate that event. It arises when an experience exceeds the psyche's capacity to symbolize it and no one is there to offer the care that would help make it understandable.

A child who is repeatedly left alone may not consciously remember every incident, but the child's body registers the feeling of abandonment. The nervous system learns that the world is not trustworthy. An emotional memory forms independently of any verbal narrative.

When there is no adult to name the pain, the child must find an explanation. Here one of the most profound movements of the child's psyche emerges: the internalization of blame.

## The Child Who Takes the Blame

For a child, parents are absolute points of reference. Admitting that the people on whom the child depends are flawed or negligent threatens their sense of survival. It is psychologically safer to conclude, "I am the problem."

This is how the silent belief of unworthiness is born:

> *"I was not loved because I am unlovable."*
>
> *"I was left because I do not deserve anyone's presence."*
>
> *"I was rejected because there is something wrong with me."*

This internalized guilt structures the superego as a critical agency that watches, accuses, and demands constant reparation. The person grows up trying to compensate for a deficiency that is not real but was felt as an absolute truth. They live in pursuit of approval, performance, impeccable religiosity, or extreme self-sufficiency as a way of paying an imaginary emotional debt.

From a theological perspective, this dynamic reveals something profound: the human experience of psychological guilt often becomes entangled with moral and spiritual consciousness. The person begins to perceive God through the filter of their traumatic history. If the earthly father was absent, God may seem distant. If he was harsh, God may seem punitive. If he was unpredictable, God may seem inconsistent.

The image of God becomes an extension of the internalized parental image.

## Neglect and Excess: Two Extremes, the Same Void

Curiously, both neglect and excess can produce similar wounds.

Neglect communicates: "You are not important."

Controlling excess communicates: "You are not capable."

Disorganized permissiveness communicates: "You are not worthy of guidance."

In every case, the wounded core is the same: a fragile identity. When that identity is not consolidated, the individual looks outside for what was not found within. They seek recognition, power, symbiotic relationships, or performative spirituality. They may become dependent on validation or rigidly self-sufficient. In either case, it is the same child trying to survive.

## The Spiritual Dimension of the Wound

The biblical tradition affirms that a human being is an indivisible unity of body, soul, and spirit. There is no ontological fragmentation between the psychological and the spiritual. Emotional wounds therefore reverberate through the experience of faith. A person who learned to repress emotions may have difficulty experiencing intimacy with God. Someone who developed rigid defenses may fear spiritual vulnerability. Someone who internalized excessive guilt may confuse grace with merit.

Yet the central message of Christianity points toward a movement opposite to defensive self-sufficiency: reconciliation and adoption. The Christian faith portrays God not as a magnified extension of flawed parental figures, but as a Father who restores identity. If trauma fragmented, grace integrates.

If guilt distorted, love redefines. If fear structured defenses, trust opens space for new emotional experiences.

This does not mean denying the need for psychological treatment. On the contrary, authentic faith encourages truth. Facing the truth includes recognizing one's wounds, dismantling internalized beliefs, and allowing the inner child to be heard.

## Healing as Integration

Healing is not a naive return to childhood, but its mature integration. It means allowing the inner child, once silenced or blamed, to be welcomed by adult consciousness and spiritual hope. Psychologically, this involves revisiting beliefs formed in pain, developing new relational experiences, and learning to distinguish the past from the present. Spiritually, it involves giving new meaning to the image of God, understanding grace as a gift, and abandoning the logic of compensation.

True maturity does not eliminate the inner child; it becomes reconciled with that child. Faith does not replace the psychic process; it gives that process direction and meaning. Human beings carry within themselves the memory of what they experienced even before they could speak. They also carry the mark of the guilt they assumed in order to survive emotionally. Between trauma and transcendence, they build their identity. When neglect or excess wounds childhood, defensive structures form and shape the whole of existence. These structures can distort relationships, spirituality, and self-image. Yet they do not constitute an irrevocable destiny. Psychoanalysis teaches us that what has been repressed can be brought into consciousness. Christian theology announces that what has been wounded can be redeemed. Both converge in the same direction: integration.

Healing means allowing the inner child to stop carrying guilt that never belonged to them. It means rebuilding one's self-image in the light of a love that does not depend on performance. It means understanding that frailty is not condemnation, but the meeting place between the human condition and grace.

Only when the wounded child finds psychological and spiritual acceptance can the adult finally live, not defensively, but freely.

## The Formation of Identity Begins Before Birth

If childhood wounds leave such deep marks, we must recognize that the process of identity formation begins even before birth. From life in the womb onward, the human being does not exist in isolation. The fetus inhabits a relational, emotional, and symbolic field that precedes and surrounds it: the mother's body, her history, her feelings, her fears, her hopes, and the context in which she lives. The womb is not merely a biological space for gestation, but a psychic and relational environment where the first inscriptions of human experience begin to be traced.

Psychoanalysis understands the subject as constituted in relationship with the other from before birth. Authors such as Freud, Winnicott, Bion, and Françoise Dolto point out that the baby is sensitive to emotional variations in the maternal environment. The fetus receives information from the external world mediated by the mother's emotional experience. Winnicott describes the mother as the baby's *environment*: before there is a structured "self," there is an experience of the continuity or disruption of being. Chronic anxiety, fear, violence, abandonment, or intense stress experienced by the mother are not merely external events; they become shared bodily experiences. The fetus does not cognitively "understand," but it *feels*, and this primitive feeling is organized as implicit bodily memory, forming the foundation of what may later manifest as security or anguish, trust or hypervigilance.

Wilfred Bion expands this understanding through the mother's *containing function*: she emotionally metabolizes the baby's raw experiences and returns them in a tolerable form. When this function is overloaded or fails, the baby may be exposed to unsymbolized emotional states that remain as psychic marks. Thus, the external environment reaches the fetus not directly, but through the way the mother lives, feels, and gives meaning to the world.

### The "Good Enough" Environment

The individual is not born complete; they are constituted through relationship with the environment. Freud indicated that the psyche is organized from the earliest experiences of pleasure and displeasure, especially in the bond with caregivers. Radically dependent, the baby receives not only nourishment from the environment, but also meanings: the tone of a voice, the gaze that welcomes or ignores, predictability or chaos.

Winnicott described the *good enough environment* as one that supports, protects, and responds sensitively to initial needs. This is not about perfection, but about a presence consistent enough for the child to develop basic trust. When the environment repeatedly fails, the subject learns to adapt in order to survive, and this adaptation often comes at the cost of spontaneity.

Some environments function like a psychic womb: places where words are firm without being violent, where boundaries do not humiliate, and where love is not conditional. In these contexts, the subject learns that the world can be trusted.

### David: Overcoming

The episode of David's anointing is paradigmatic. When the prophet Samuel visits Jesse's house, the older brothers are presented first. David is not even summoned. He remains in the field, invisible. This initial exclusion is not merely a narrative detail, but a symbolic structure in the constitution of his subjectivity: the unseen son, the one passed over, the one whose identity finds no mirror in familial recognition.

From a psychoanalytic perspective, the formation of the self depends profoundly on the gaze of the other. The absence of that gaze can produce feelings of inadequacy, insecurity, and a relentless search for validation. Real or perceived primary rejection often becomes a matrix for compensatory behaviors. Yet the same trauma can become a driving force for creative processing when the subject finds a new symbolic place that reorganizes their identity.

In David's case, this new place emerges at the moment of his anointing. What he lacked in paternal recognition is bestowed upon him through divine election. Biblical theology affirms that God sees the heart, not outward appearance. Psychologically, such an experience is equivalent to internalizing an absolute gaze that validates the subject in their uniqueness. Rejection is not denied; it is given new meaning.

Thus, the wound does not disappear, but it ceases to be determinative. Trauma is integrated into the personal narrative, not as a sentence of inferiority, but as the setting for a calling.

### Joseph: Affection That Sustains in Adversity

Joseph, in the book of Genesis, grows up amid rivalry and injustice, but carries within him the marks of a formative affection received from his father. Even when thrown into a cistern, sold as a slave, and forgotten in prison, Joseph does not lose his capacity to symbolize suffering. His early environment did not immunize him against pain, but it gave him internal resources that kept him from confusing himself with it. His story illustrates how an emotionally secure environment in the early years can provide a foundation for passing through the most severe crises without losing one's identity.

### Jephthah: When Rejection Shapes Identity

By contrast, the story of Jephthah, told in Judges 11, begins not with a calling, but with a wound. Before becoming a judge in Israel, Jephthah was a rejected son. The text is direct:

> *"Jephthah the Gileadite was a mighty man of valor, but he was the son of a prostitute."* (Judges 11:1)

This mark is not merely biographical; it becomes social, symbolic, and bound up with his identity. Jephthah grows up in an environment where his origins place him on the margins. When Gilead's legitimate sons become adults, they drive him away:

> *"You shall not inherit in our father's house, for you are the son of another woman."* (Judges 11:2)

Here the core of the trauma is established: rejection, expulsion, and a rupture in belonging. Jephthah loses not only his material inheritance, but also the right to exist within his own family. The environment that should have provided protection and a name becomes hostile, exclusionary, and violent.

The text recounts that Jephthah flees to the land of Tob, where "worthless men joined him" (Judges 11:3). Psychologically, this reveals a common pattern in people rejected early in life: the search for belonging in marginal settings. When the family rejects someone, that individual finds acceptance wherever it is possible, even in unstable or morally ambiguous environments.

Jephthah's identity begins to be shaped not by care, but by the need for self-defense. He becomes a "mighty man," a warrior not only by vocation, but by adaptation. Hostile environments produce hypervigilant, combative, and often emotionally rigid individuals.

When the elders of Gilead seek him out to lead the war against the Ammonites, Jephthah responds with distrust:

> *"Did you not hate me and drive me out of my father's house? Why have you come to me now?"* (Judges 11:7)

His words reveal a psyche marked by rejection: the past has not healed. Jephthah agrees to help, but he demands guarantees. He needs formal recognition, power, and position. He does not trust the relationship; he trusts the contract, the bargain. This same logic appears in his relationship with God. Before the battle, he makes an extreme vow:

> *"If You deliver the people of Ammon into my hands, whoever comes out of the doors of my house... shall be the Lord's."* (Judges 11:30-31)

Here we see a profound consequence of living in a hostile environment: difficulty trusting in grace. Jephthah relates to God as someone who must negotiate, bargain, and offer something in order to secure acceptance. His vow reveals a spirituality marked by emotional insecurity.

The outcome is tragic: Jephthah's vow culminates in the death of his own daughter. A decision made out of woundedness produces irreparable consequences. Jephthah wins the war but loses what matters most. The environment of rejection that drove him out is tragically reproduced in his own home. The biblical text seems to suggest that unresolved trauma tends to repeat itself, not because of conscious malice, but because healing has not taken place.

*The stories of Joseph, Jephthah, and David show us how the early environment can structure or fragment identity. But what happens when that identity begins to be constructed consciously? Who are we, in fact, behind the masks life has made us wear? In the next chapter, we will delve into the fundamental question of identity, from God's original design in creation to the forces that distort it throughout life.*

# Chapter 3: The Construction of Identity

## The Question That Haunts Us

In the previous chapters, we came to understand how childhood shapes the emotional patterns we carry throughout life and how our earliest wounds, some of them predating birth itself, are inscribed upon the human soul. Now we arrive at an inescapable question: *Who am I?* This question is not merely philosophical; it is profoundly existential and spiritual. The answer we give to it, or the answer we receive from others, determines how we live, how we relate to others, and how we see our place in the world.

Inspired by Friedrich Nietzsche's words, *"Become who you are,"* an unknown author offered the following reflection:

> *"But who am I? Ever since I was little, people have told me who I should be. A good girl, responsible, strong, well-behaved, intelligent, but not overly sensitive or weak. I collected versions of myself the way one wears borrowed clothes. Some fit for a long time. Others were so tight that they left invisible marks.*
>
> *Then I heard that phrase: become who you are. But how can I become something I still do not know how to name? Who am I beyond what others expect of me? Who am I when no one is watching? Who am I when I am not trying to please anyone? When I am not trying to survive, when I am not trying to convince.*
>
> *Maybe I am not a finished definition. Maybe I am only a process, a territory being discovered, a book that writes itself as it goes. Maybe becoming who I am does not mean discovering a fixed identity, but having the courage to remove the identities others placed upon me. It is not about inventing new things for myself, but remembering who I truly am.*
>
> *Remembering the girl who felt the whole world in her chest. Remembering the woman who still dreams, even when she is tired. Remembering the voice that existed before the fears.*
>
> *Who am I? I am what remains when the masks fall. I am what persists, even after the falls. I am only one of the versions that died, and the courage of the one being born.*
>
> *Become who you are is not a destination. It is a calling. And perhaps the answer will not come in words, but in choices, in silences, in necessary departures. I do not need to know exactly who I am before I begin to become. I just need to stop running away."*

This reflection precisely captures the dilemma of identity: most of us do not know who we truly are because we have been buried beneath versions imposed from the outside, by family, culture, and wounds. As we will see, the construction of identity is both a psychological process and a spiritual project.

## Identity in the Process of Creation

From a biblical perspective, human identity begins with the creation of Adam and Eve. In the book of Genesis, we read:

> *"Then God said, 'Let Us make man in Our image, according to Our likeness.'"* (Genesis 1:26)

This foundational verse reveals that human identity is rooted in the fact that we were created in the *image of God*, the theological concept known as *imago Dei*. This means that, as human beings, we reflect the divine nature and possess a connection with the Creator that confers intrinsic value upon every life.

The concept of being made "in the image and likeness of God" implies that human identity is spiritual in its essence, with the capacity to reflect God's attributes: reason, creativity, morality, and the capacity to love. This bond with God is the central point of human identity because it is through this bond that we find our value, our dignity, and our purpose.

### The Fall and Separation from True Identity

With original sin, described in Genesis 3, human identity was distorted. By disobeying God, Adam and Eve not only broke the perfect relationship they had with the Creator, but also became disconnected from their true identity. From that moment onward, humanity began to live in a state of spiritual alienation, seeking satisfaction in fleeting things and often outside the divine plan, just as our society reflects today.

Sin brought with it the loss of original innocence and the arrival of a new concept of identity, marked by selfishness, shame, and separation from God, since before sin they were naked and felt no shame. Instead of seeing themselves as the image of God and living according to their purpose, human beings began to live egocentrically, often seeking identity in success, power, possessions, or outside influences.

Human identity is undoubtedly one of the most challenging and enriching questions the human experience offers. It is built throughout life and influenced by many factors, from individual psychology to the cultural and social forces around us. Identity is not fixed, but a constantly evolving process in which each individual has the opportunity to discover, reinvent, and, above all, find themselves. Ultimately, the construction of identity is the search for a "self" that is authentic, meaningful, and connected to others.

## The Role of the Father Figure in Identity Formation

Before a child is born, even before receiving a name, the main question the world asks is, "Boy or girl?" The sex assigned at birth, although biological, carries a symbolic weight that begins to shape identity from the first moments of existence. Sex, however, is only the starting point of a much more complex process in which the earliest external influences, especially the father figure, play a crucial role.

Psychologically speaking, the father represents to the child the principle of authority, differentiation from the mother, and, in a fundamental way, validation of what the child will become and how the child will see themselves in the future. The mother, by contrast, is responsible for transmitting and developing affection, tenderness, nourishment, organization, welcome, and relationship in the child. Identity is defined largely by the relationships the child establishes with the people around them. The father has the task of affirming and validating the identity of both son and daughter, helping them understand not only who they are, but also their purpose in the world, because he is the one who will establish healthy boundaries.

### Representation for Sons and Daughters

For a son, the father is often seen as a model to follow, a figure of autonomy, strength, and assertiveness. He is envisioned as the one who will pass on the traditions, teaching his son what it means to be a man and what is expected of him as a mature and responsible man. The father becomes the principal mirror through which the son understands his masculinity and feels capable of taking an active role in the world.

For a daughter, the father fulfills a different but equally essential function. He is the first male figure with whom the girl has a relationship and therefore has enormous influence over the way she will see herself as a woman. The way the father treats his daughter and her mother, and how he validates and recognizes his daughter, is fundamental to the construction of feminine self-esteem. The relationship with her father can directly influence the way the daughter relates to other men throughout life: whether she feels valued or needs to seek elsewhere the validation she should have received at home.

For both son and daughter, the father's absence or failure to offer affirmation can generate distortions in identity. For a son, this can result in difficulty affirming himself as a man. For a daughter, it can result in difficulty seeing herself as worthy of love and respect. The father's presence, on the other hand, offers the child a solid foundation of trust and recognition.

### Research on Father Absence and Sexual Orientation

Following an informal study based on evidence provided by sexuality specialists who closely follow this reality, as well as by people themselves who identify as homosexual, the author found that more than 90% of the reported cases indicate some kind of trauma related to the father figure, whether from the father's absence, excessive behaviors, or physical, verbal, or sexual abuse. The experience with this figure appears to have a profound impact on identity formation and, in some cases, may influence sexual orientation. The father's emotional or physical absence may leave a significant gap in a child's development, while abuse committed by him, or even by the mother or siblings, also leaves scars that reverberate throughout life, shaping how the person relates to others and to their own sexual identity.

It is important to remember, however, that sexual orientation is a multifaceted phenomenon involving not only trauma or parental absence, but also biological, social, and cultural factors. No matter how significant the influence of the father figure may be, it does not definitively determine a person's sexual orientation. Every story is unique, and the construction of sexual identity is a profound and individual process in which the interaction between the individual and their surrounding environment plays a significant role.

### The Affirmation of Jesus' Identity by the Heavenly Father

To better understand the impact of paternal affirmation upon identity, we can look at the life of Jesus and the moments when God the Father affirms Him as His Son. Even as the Son of God, Jesus was not immune to the need for paternal affirmation. At key moments in His life, the Father made His voice heard to reaffirm Jesus' identity, making clear who He was and what His mission would be.

**The Baptism (Matthew 3:16-17):** As Jesus emerged from the waters of the Jordan, the voice of God the Father was heard: *"This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased."* This moment of public affirmation was fundamental to the formation of Jesus' identity. In being affirmed by the Father, He received not only confirmation of who He was, but also validation of His mission on earth. It was not merely a declaration of paternity, but an affirmation of purpose and identity.

**The Transfiguration (Matthew 17:5):** At another significant moment, when Jesus appeared in His glory before Peter, James, and John, the voice of God was heard again: *"This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Hear Him."* This affirmation reinforces the recognition of Jesus as the Son of God and highlights His authority.

These moments are crucial to understanding that, even though He was divine, Jesus needed paternal affirmation to fulfill His mission with courage and conviction. In the same way, sons and daughters need the affirmation of their parents to understand fully their identity and their place in the world.

## The Influence of the Environment on the Construction of Identity

The construction of human identity is a continuous and dynamic process, profoundly influenced by the social and cultural environment in which we live. From the earliest years of life, children begin to interact with the world around them: family, school, neighborhood, and electronic devices. Each of these spaces contributes significantly to the formation of how children perceive themselves and their role in the world.

At school, children encounter a new set of influences. Teachers, classmates, social norms, and the expectations that arise in this environment are fundamental to the socialization process. Children learn to compare themselves with others, conform to collective behaviors, and identify with groups that share similar values.

The social environment also has a profound impact. A child who grows up in poverty or on the margins of society may develop a limited view of themselves, nourished by the belief that they are incapable of reaching certain goals. This feeling of inadequacy can leave deep marks, producing a fragile identity that has difficulty integrating and asserting itself.

It is not only observed behavior or spoken words that have power in the construction of identity, but also the values children are taught to live by. From a very early age, parents and caregivers transmit what they consider important, such as honesty, respect for authority, and care for others, or, in some cases, an emphasis on competition and personal achievement. These values become the lenses through which the child interprets the world.

As globalization advances, cultural boundaries begin to dissolve, creating new forms of identity expression. Social media plays a crucial role in this process, enabling connections around the world and creating a constant negotiation between the "local self" and the "global self."

### Carl Rogers' Concept of Congruence

Psychology helps us understand how all these factors interact in the formation of identity. Carl Rogers, an important humanistic psychologist, introduced the concept of *congruence*: the alignment between the *real self* (what we actually are) and the *ideal self* (what we aspire to be). When congruence is present, a person feels authentic and at peace with themselves. Otherwise, *incongruence* arises, which can lead to anxiety and emotional disturbances. Congruence is seen as one of the principal elements of personal growth and mental health.

When people cannot integrate the different parts of themselves, internal conflicts arise, such as the discrepancy between the "real self" and the "ideal self," generating anguish and suffering. In this sense, the search for authenticity is an attempt to achieve congruence between what we feel internally and what we express externally.

Throughout life, identity may be challenged by changes and adversity, including transitions between stages of life, existential crises, and losses. Each of these moments requires a renegotiation of identity, a reevaluation of who we are in relation to the world and to others.

Finally, identity is not only about who we are in the present, but also about who we wish to become and how we see ourselves in relation to the future. The search for purpose and for a greater meaning to our existence is a fundamental part of this process. In a constantly changing world, identity becomes an anchor that helps us preserve our integrity, while also serving as a reflection of our experiences and a beacon that illuminates our path.

*We now understand how identity is constructed, from God's original design in creation, through paternal influence, to the forces of the environment that shape us. But what happens when this identity is built upon foundations of pain? What happens when childhood experiences produce not only insecurity, but deep beliefs that begin invisibly to govern our choices? In the next chapter, we will explore the formation of limiting beliefs, the "invisible glasses" through which we see the world, and how they become false prophets that distort our perception of reality, of ourselves, and of God.*

# Chapter 4: Limiting Beliefs and False Prophets

In the previous chapters, we explored how childhood functions as fertile ground for the formation of identity, how defense mechanisms take root in the face of pain, and how the wounded inner child continues to operate in adult life. We now move into a central dimension of this process: limiting beliefs, the internal structures of thought that, once formed, begin silently to govern our decisions, our relationships, and our perception of ourselves. Alongside them, we will examine the figures who function as "false prophets," people who, consciously or unconsciously, planted these beliefs in our emotional soil.

## Decisions Guided by Invisible Patterns

How many of our decisions have been guided by patterns the world imposed on us? How often have we been led to follow fragmented emotions, disordered desires, and wishes incompatible with God's purposes? How many choices have we made based on fears and pains that are still active in the unconscious, without even realizing how much they influence us?

Imagine someone who was frequently rejected as a child and did not receive the necessary attention and affection. As this person grows, they become extremely careful to avoid any situation in which they might feel rejected again. Throughout adult life, they avoid becoming deeply involved with others because they carry a fear of being abandoned or ignored, exactly as happened in childhood.

Even if this person rationally knows that the people around them are trustworthy and that there is no real threat of rejection, something within still prevents them from opening up completely. They end up making decisions that keep them distant from others, avoiding close relationships or opportunities for professional growth, even when those situations could bring them happiness and fulfillment.

The conception of the world we create within ourselves is often shaped by pain, rejection, trauma, and fragmented experiences. In this way, we form an "inner world" that may be profoundly misaligned with the standards of the Kingdom of God. This is how patterns of living form without our realizing it, and we cling to behaviors because, from our limited perspective, we believe they are best.

## When Personal History Shapes Patterns

A man once told me that he had great difficulty trusting women. This affected his relationships, which were sometimes superficial, and made it very hard for him to give himself fully or form deep and lasting bonds. He sabotaged his relationships so that they never reached a certain depth. Unconsciously, he would pull away.

As I talked with him further, I learned that when he was about four years old, his mother had abandoned him after his parents separated. In that environment, he witnessed fights and arguments during a period marked by family chaos in which emotional instability, fear, and insecurity prevailed.

When his mother left home, his grandmother took her place. His grandmother, however, suffered from alcoholism and also behaved aggressively and authoritatively, constantly beating him in an attempt to "educate" her grandson. His father, though an upright and loyal man, was emotionally distant and also turned to alcohol in an attempt to relieve his inner pain, often using drinking as an emotional escape. Concerned with providing for the family, he focused on work and was unable to connect with his son's emotions in a functional and healthy way.

A short time later, the man's mother and grandmother died.

The stage had been set. The figures who should have offered affection, tenderness, love, nourishment, security, protection, validation, and comfort, all structures of a healthy relationship, did not fulfill that role effectively. The child learned that these bonds brought him insecurity. From this dynamic, amid chaos and an unstable, unsafe environment with little emotional connection, he unconsciously learned that loving and relating to others was too dangerous. He normalized chaos, reproducing chaotic and stressful moments in his relationships and creating conflict. His unconscious reproduced the "familiar hells," attempting to bring into adult life a pattern established in childhood.

This child registered in his inner system that forming bonds was too dangerous, given the pain of abandonment and rejection he had suffered at the hands of female authority figures. Throughout his life, he built defensive patterns to avoid being hurt in intimate relationships. He internalized a punitive way of relating emotionally: he would enter a relationship and soon withdraw, leaving emotional devastation and the wounds of romantic disillusionment in the other person. When a breakup seemed imminent, which often occurred only in his imagination, he acted first, sabotaging the relationship so that he would not have to suffer yet another pain, yet another rejection.

These behaviors do not occur because the person wants to be unhappy, but because, on some deep level, they are trying to protect themselves from the same pains of the past. The fear of rejection forged in childhood continues to influence their choices, leading them to seek safety in withdrawal, even if that means sacrificing good things that life could offer.

## Internal Schemas: The Invisible Glasses

Psychology recognizes this phenomenon as the construction of *internal schemas*, cognitive structures that, when formed out of suffering and emotional neglect, lead to distorted interpretations of reality (Young, 2003). Schemas are patterns of thought and belief that structure our perceptions and actions, functioning as mental shortcuts that help us process information quickly. For example, if someone has a "distrust" schema, that person will probably interpret the actions of others as deceptive or malicious even when the evidence does not support that interpretation. This simplifying mechanism is useful, but it can also be flawed and distorted, especially when based on negative or traumatic experiences.

We can imagine this phenomenon as the construction of invisible glasses through which we come to see the world. These "glasses" begin to form very early, especially in moments of pain, rejection, or emotional neglect. When a person suffers repeatedly, their mind tries to make sense of the experience in order to survive it. The mind therefore creates internal interpretations about the self, other people, and life.

If someone grows up feeling unseen, unheard, or unwelcomed, they may come to believe that they are unimportant or unworthy of love. Over time, this idea ceases to be merely a thought and begins to function as a fixed lens: everything that happens afterward is interpreted through it, forming a pattern of distorted thoughts and structures of functioning.

In this way, silence may be understood as rejection, correction may sound like humiliation, and a boundary may be experienced as abandonment. Reality has not changed, but the way it is interpreted has been shaped by the wounds of the past. The person is not reacting to what is happening now, but to what it awakens within them based on earlier experiences.

Transformation begins when the person realizes that these lenses are not who they are, but something constructed during a time of pain. By recognizing this, they can gradually remove the glasses and learn to see life with greater clarity, freedom, and truth.

## Ana's Story

Let us imagine the story of Ana, a young woman who went through a painful situation in childhood: her family constantly criticized her, making her feel that she was never good enough. Those words of reproach and gestures of disapproval became etched in her mind, and even after many years, she still remembers the criticism as if it were yesterday.

Ana's trauma was not merely a passing memory, but something that left a deep mark within her. She began to internalize those words and attitudes as though they were the truth about who she was. Over time, she began to believe that she did not have enough worth to be loved or respected and that her actions would always be inadequate. These beliefs began to govern the way she saw herself and interacted with the world around her.

Based on these beliefs, Ana developed patterns of behavior that were, in fact, attempts to protect herself from the pain she had felt. She began to avoid exposing herself in situations where she might be criticized, such as presentations at work or new relationships. The fear of failure and rejection was always present, preventing her from trying new things. Even though she was capable of taking on new challenges, the belief that she was not good enough kept her from acting.

This process of creating limiting beliefs is natural when trauma occurs because the human mind seeks a way to protect itself from what caused pain. When something deeply negative happens, especially in childhood, the brain tends to create a "reading" of the world based on that experience. This can lead to the formation of beliefs such as "I am not good enough" or "I do not deserve to be happy." Over time, these beliefs become part of one's identity and begin to influence choices, actions, and even the opportunities we seek or create.

The problem is that these beliefs often do not correspond to reality. They are distortions created by the pain and fear of the past. Over the years, Ana, like many of us, makes decisions based on the belief that she cannot be successful or loved when, in fact, she has all the qualities needed to attain what she desires. Without realizing it, she creates a vicious cycle in which her actions confirm her limiting beliefs, and those beliefs reinforce her patterns of avoidance.

These are learned behaviors, mechanisms created when the *reptilian brain*, the most primitive part of our nervous system, activates fight-or-flight mode to ensure survival. At first, this is an ingenious response by the brain. The serious problem arises when these behaviors do not switch off and remain constantly active in the emotional system. We must understand that some behaviors were valid at that moment. In those circumstances, they were the best we could do to escape the pain. Now, however, we need to face things differently by acting instead of continuing to react to a painful stimulus.

The good news is that, although these beliefs and behavioral patterns may form after trauma, they can be challenged and changed. The first step is to recognize that these beliefs are not absolute truth and that the human mind has the capacity to be renewed (Romans 12:2). Through self-awareness, emotional support, and practices that strengthen self-esteem, it is possible to dismantle these limiting beliefs and create new ways of thinking and acting that are more closely aligned with each person's true capacity and potential.

Thus, what once seemed to be an inner prison can become an opportunity for growth and transformation.

## The Main Limiting Beliefs

Let us look at how some dysfunctional behavioral structures are built, and how beliefs begin to germinate in the soil of childhood.

### "I Am Not Good Enough"

This belief may form when a child receives constant criticism or does not feel enough approval from parents or important figures, leading the child to believe that they will never be able to meet a standard of acceptance.

### "I Do Not Deserve to Be Loved"

This belief arises in contexts of emotional rejection, abandonment, or emotional neglect. The child begins to believe that their value is not recognized and that they cannot be loved or accepted by others.

### "I Need to Be Perfect to Be Accepted"

If a child grows up in an environment where perfection is expected, or is constantly corrected without receiving support, they may internalize the belief that they will be accepted only if everything they do is flawless, forming a perfectionistic adult.

### "The World Is a Dangerous Place"

This belief develops in an unstable or unsafe environment where the child is exposed to abuse, violence, or unpredictability, leading the child to believe that the world is threatening and that they must constantly protect themselves.

### "I Am Responsible for Other People's Problems"

This belief forms in dysfunctional families where the child feels that they must care emotionally for their parents or others around them, even when doing so overwhelms their own needs.

### "I Am Not Intelligent Enough"

This belief may be created when a child has difficulty at school or is frequently compared unfavorably with others, leading the child to believe that they are incapable of learning or excelling.

### "I Cannot Trust People"

This belief is generated by trauma related to abandonment, betrayal, or dishonesty by people close to the child, resulting in a pattern of distrust toward others.

### "I Will Always Fail"

This belief forms through repeated experiences of failure, criticism, or negative comparisons during childhood, causing the child to believe that they are incapable of succeeding at anything, regardless of their efforts.

### "I Cannot Express My Emotions"

This belief develops in environments where the child's emotions were ignored, ridiculed, or punished, creating the belief that it is neither safe nor acceptable to be vulnerable or express feelings.

### "I Have No Control Over My Life"

This belief may result from an environment in which the child feels powerless, such as situations of abuse or neglect, where there is no room for them to exercise autonomy or make decisions about themselves.

These limiting beliefs can become so deep and ingrained that, throughout life, they influence choices, behavior, and even how a person sees themselves in the world. Dismantling them and creating new beliefs that foster growth and well-being often requires a process of self-knowledge and overcoming these patterns.

## The False Prophets in Our Lives

In the biblical context, the term "prophet" refers to a person chosen and called by God to convey His message to the people. The word comes from the Greek *prophetes*, meaning "one who speaks on behalf of another," specifically, on behalf of God. The prophet's mission is to be a divine messenger, communicating God's will, warning of spiritual dangers, announcing judgments, and at the same time proclaiming hope and restoration.

This can also represent the role of the authority figures who pass through our lives. Our authority figures should often fulfill the role of God's messengers, bringing life through their words and their message to those under their influence. Yet we often see a figure who should bring a message of comfort, authority, encouragement, and approval do the opposite, becoming a *false prophet* who brings words of destruction, discouragement, obstruction, and emotional death.

In the biblical and psychological context, a "false prophet" can be understood not only as someone who distorts religious or spiritual truth, but also as a person who consciously or unconsciously influences others negatively, creating limiting beliefs that imprison them in a cycle of suffering and deception. Limiting beliefs are often formed from life experiences, especially during childhood and adolescence. Certain people encountered along this journey can have a significant impact on the formation of these beliefs. Let us consider the main figures.

### Parents or Caregivers

**Negative influence:** Parents who constantly criticize, compare, or ignore their children's emotional needs may create the belief that a child is not good enough, does not deserve love, or must be perfect to be accepted.

**Positive influence:** Parents who encourage self-confidence, teach that mistakes are part of learning, and offer unconditional love help form beliefs in healthy self-esteem and the ability to face challenges.

### Teachers

**Negative influence:** A teacher who devalues a student's contributions, makes negative comparisons, or ignores a child's learning difficulties may reinforce beliefs of inadequacy or inability.

**Positive influence:** Educators who recognize individual talents, offer support and encouragement to improve skills, and help children develop confidence in their abilities can foster beliefs in competence and personal worth.

### Friends and Peers

**Negative influence:** Friends or peers who mock, marginalize, or ridicule someone may create or reinforce beliefs that the person is unworthy of friendship or not good enough to relate to others.

**Positive influence:** Healthy relationships and friendships that demonstrate support, acceptance, and respect help form beliefs in belonging, trust, and the ability to build meaningful connections.

### Authority Figures (Religious Leaders, Mentors, Coaches)

**Negative influence:** Leaders or mentors who impose rigid standards, make unattainable demands, or induce feelings of guilt and shame can create beliefs that the person will never be "good enough" or will always fail.

**Positive influence:** Leaders who encourage positive reflection, self-compassion, and forgiveness help build a growth mindset and resilience.

### Siblings or Close Relatives

**Negative influence:** Siblings or other family members who compete in a hostile way, provoke jealousy, or belittle one another's achievements may foster beliefs of insecurity, envy, or an inability to succeed.

**Positive influence:** Family members who offer mutual support, celebrate victories, and show understanding during difficulties can strengthen the belief that it is possible to grow, improve, and be loved.

### Romantic or Marital Relationships

**Negative influence:** Partners who manipulate, devalue, or emotionally abuse a person can create beliefs that the person does not deserve to be treated with respect or that their needs and desires are irrelevant.

**Positive influence:** Healthy and respectful romantic relationships foster beliefs in self-esteem, mutual trust, and the ability to love and be loved in a healthy way.

### Society and the Media

**Negative influence:** Social pressure, including standards of beauty, success, and behavior widely promoted by the media, can create beliefs of inadequacy or dissatisfaction with one's own appearance, ability, or status.

**Positive influence:** Campaigns that promote diversity, inclusion, and the importance of self-knowledge help form positive beliefs about one's own value, regardless of external expectations.

### Professional Authorities (Therapists, Counselors)

**Negative influence:** Therapists or counselors who invalidate feelings or label a person in derogatory ways may reinforce beliefs of inability or of being unworthy of help.

**Positive influence:** Professionals who listen attentively, validate emotions, and help people find their own strength contribute to the formation of beliefs in empowerment and confidence.

### Role Models (Celebrities, Idols, Social Leaders)

**Negative influence:** The idolization of public figures who promote unrealistic standards of perfection can cause harmful comparisons and beliefs that these ideals are impossible to attain.

**Positive influence:** Public figures who share stories of overcoming, failure, and vulnerability can teach that it is possible to grow, learn, and change throughout life.

## An Invitation to Trust

In many situations, the influence of these people, when combined with personal experiences and cultural contexts, contributes to the formation of limiting beliefs. By becoming aware of these influences and working over time to dismantle them, a person can break free from limiting patterns and build a new narrative for their life.

> *"Commit your way to the Lord; trust in Him, and He will act."* (Psalm 37:5)

This verse is an invitation to faith and rest. It means transferring our worries, anxieties, and difficulties to God and trusting in His providence instead of trying to control everything on our own. Surrendering is not giving up; it is trusting that God will take control of what is impossible for us.

*If limiting beliefs are the fruit of what we have learned, it is natural to ask: how, after all, do we learn? How did the models around us in childhood shape not only our behavior, but the very structure of our thought? In the next chapter, we will explore the mechanisms of social learning and modeling, the process through which we absorb, imitate, and internalize the patterns of those around us, for better and for worse.*
